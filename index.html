The problem

A warehouse full of value nobody can borrow against

Green coffee sits in storage for six to twelve months. It is a real asset with a public price, a measurable grade and a known shelf life — and yet it is almost never financed at its true worth. Four reasons.

01

Dead capital

Six to twelve months of inventory funded entirely by working capital. The cost of carry is paid in cash flow, not accounted for.

02

Unpriced quality

An 86-point lot and an 81-point lot are haircut at the same rate. Quality is talked about, never converted into a number a lender can use.

03

Unmeasured decay

Nobody knows what a month at 28°C actually costs. It costs about 14% of pledgeable value a year — and it is invisible.

04

Unbankable receipts

Paper receipts, no registry, no independent audit, no discharge control. Lenders have learned to price that at zero.

Architecture

Three layers, one asset class

The vault is the easy part. What makes a receipt bankable is the valuation layer on top of it and the compliance layer that stops it from being sold twice.

Layer 1

Custody — 18°C, 55% RH, sealed lots

Batch-level lots, sample retention for 18 months, position tags cross-verified against the digital passport.

V = P × Q × D(t)
Layer 2

Valuation — four factors, every day

Spot anchor, quality multiplier, temperature-adjusted decay, volatility haircut. The output is a loan, not an opinion.

LTV = 1 − VaR − costs
Layer 3

Compliance — three locks, all mandatory

Registry number, independent audit, pledge state machine. Remove any one and the receipt silently becomes an unsecured loan.

Spread alone does not carry this business. Turnover does: the same position turning three times a year earns three times, and the disposal channel is what makes the third turn possible.

Why our receipts price higher

The last row is the one competitors cannot copy. A bank that has to sell a defaulted lot on the open market charges for that discount; a bank that owns the roaster and the café does not.
